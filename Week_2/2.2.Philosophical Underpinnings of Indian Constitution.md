# Philosophical Underpinnings of Indian Constitution

**Week - 2**

## The Six Pillars of Constitutional Philosophy

### Individual Freedom

1. Raja Ram Mohan Roy fought for press freedom in the early 1800s
2. Indians protested the infamous Rowlatt Act that allowed arbitrary arrests
3. Every freedom in our Constitution has a story of struggle behind it.

### Social Justice

1. Reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes

### Unity in Diversity

1. Minority rights to establish educational institutions, or special status for certain regions like Nagaland under Article 371A.

### Secularism

1. Separation of state from religious institutions

### Universal Franchise

1. Every adult citizen can vote (1950)

### Federalism

1. Asymmetric federalism - giving different states different arrangements based on their specific needs - allows for unity without uniformity.

## Indian Constitutional Thinkers

1. The constitution reflects Gandhi’s Moral Philosophy
   - a. Swaraj and Ethics in Politics.
2. Ambedkar’s Philosophy of Justice: Equality, Liberty, Fraternity
   - a. Annihilation of Caste (1936)
3. Nehru’s Modernist Vision: Scientific Temper and Development
   - a. IIT and the Planning Commission
   - b. Article 51A
4. Tagore’s Humanism: Unity in Diversity

## Western Influences

- Liberalism (Locke, Montesquieu, Mill) → limited government, separation of powers, natural rights (life, liberty, property) shaping Fundamental Rights
- Republicanism/Democracy (Rousseau) → popular sovereignty; US Bill of Rights → model for justiciable rights
- Socialism (Russian Revolution, Fabian socialism) → influenced Nehru and Ambedkar, reflected in the Directive Principles (redistribution, welfare, social justice)
- Humanism (UDHR 1948) → global backdrop for dignity, equality, freedom

## Indian Traditions

- Dharma → moral order and duty-based governance (Manusmriti, Arthashastra, Buddhist texts)
- Pluralism/Tolerance → drawing on Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, Sikh coexistence; echoes of Akbar's Sulh-i-Kuland Ashoka's edicts in constitutional secularism
- Bhakti/Sufi movements → egalitarianism feeding into the ideal of fraternity
- Gandhian thought → ahimsa, sarvodaya, swaraj shaping values of peace and justice, even though his village-economy vision didn't make it in

## The Four Core Ideals per Ambedkar

- Justice – social/economic, not just legal (e.g., abolition of untouchability, affirmative action)
- Liberty – positive freedoms, not just freedom from state control
- Equality – formal (Article 14) plus substantive (reservations)
- Fraternity – the most neglected, but the "glue" holding democracy together
